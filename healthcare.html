The Healthcare Apostolate

Apostolate of the Order • I

Corporal Works of Mercy

To Serve the Sick and the Suffering

Christ did not preach only. He healed. He touched the leper whom no one would touch, He raised the widow’s son at Nain, and He told us plainly that what we do to the least of these we do to Him. The healthcare apostolate of the Ordo Militantium Reginae Caeli exists because that command was given to all of us, and because a Faith that will not stoop to the bedside is no Faith at all.

The Order serves the sick and the suffering without distinction of creed, condition, or worthiness — the elderly who have been forgotten, the dying who are alone, the disabled whom a utilitarian age counts as burdens, and the unborn whose lives are held cheap. Where our members can give skilled care they give it; where they can give only presence and prayer, they give that.

This is not charity as the world understands it. It is not a transaction, nor a programme, nor a substitute for the Gospel. It is the Corporal Works of Mercy performed by Catholics who believe that the body before them is a temple and the soul within it is immortal.

The Whole Person

Body and Soul Together

Modern medicine can do extraordinary things for the body and has almost nothing to say to the soul. The Order refuses that division. Our members are formed to see the sick person whole — to bring physical comfort and spiritual consolation in the same visit, to know when to fetch a priest, and never to treat the sacraments as an afterthought at the end of a chart.

We hold without apology to the Church’s teaching on the dignity of human life from conception to natural death. We do not cooperate in euthanasia, in abortion, or in any act that treats a human life as disposable. Our members serve under that constraint gladly, because it is the only ground on which real mercy can stand.

"I was sick, and you visited me." — Matthew 25:36
How Members Serve

The Work in Practice

Members carry this apostolate according to their state in life and their competence. Some serve through the professions they already hold in medicine and nursing. Others visit the homebound and the institutionalised, sit with the dying, drive those who cannot drive themselves, or keep the vigil of prayer that sustains everyone else doing the work.

No member is asked to give what they do not have. Every member is asked to give what they do.
